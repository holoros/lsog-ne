John,

Congratulations again on the LSOG paper. Regina mentioned the July 8 field day and I would like to join if there is room; getting out with you and your team to see the stands is the right way to ground all of this. I have spent real time with your paper and your archived data, partly because of the CFRU connection, and I want to be clear up front that I read the work as careful and transparent. You are candid about the pitfalls, including that airborne LiDAR cannot cleanly separate late-successional from old-growth structure, the data and code are open, and I reproduced your random forest cleanly (94.2% against your 94.1%, with canopy cover above 15 m as the top predictor in both). I also understand the MNAP truthing shows the model under-predicts in places, which fits the larger point rather than undercutting it.

What started as a critique has become a narrower and, I hope, constructive piece, and that is the version I want you to have. The Comment makes four points, all built on your archived data and on independent public data over the same ground. First, training accuracy is high for every reasonable approach, so it cannot be what distinguishes the maps. Second, independent operationalizations of LSOG that each train well still disagree markedly on the landscape: any-LSOG ranges about 2.8-fold across your LiDAR classifier, an FIA-on-canopy-height map, and TreeMap, and only about a fifth of the flagged ground is shared. I also checked an independent Forest Service old-growth product (Pelz et al.), which disagrees about as much, so this is not an artifact of comparing your map to variants I built. Third, the canopy signal predicts large-tree, tall-canopy forest well but is largely blind to dead wood, so it maps big-tree forest, which is roughly three times more extensive than ground-defined old forest. Fourth, FIA design-based estimation puts older forest at 3.9% [3.3, 4.6] of Maine forestland and shows the stock stable to rising once you separate the gross harvest flux from the net stock. The thread through all four is that the honest unit is a range with its definition and its sampling interval attached, not a single number, before a map anchors $200-300 million in prioritization.

One thing worth flagging directly, and I have written it carefully in the Comment as a risk rather than a finding: an unverified, high-profile map can in principle create a perverse incentive to harvest mapped stands ahead of feared regulation. I have no data that this is happening and do not assert it; I raise it only as one more reason to attach uncertainty and verify before any stand is designated. The definition you choose also moves the carbon attributed to LSOG several fold, which is the same point from the stakes side.

Jereme Frank has offered to refine a side-by-side of how much LSOG each method estimates, statewide and by region. That fuller side-by-side, along with a repeatable FIA-anchored multi-axis classification and a complete cross-map accuracy assessment, is a separate manuscript I am assembling now; the Comment points to it but does not try to be it. Jereme will also see whether CFRU can give me a look under the hood of your layer, which I would welcome. You should be able to see exactly what I did, and I would rather build this with you than around you.

So, two tracks, and they are not in conflict. The first, my preference, is a grounded follow-up we do together: the cross-map accuracy assessment, the design-based baseline, and the multi-axis classification. The second is that I submit the attached as a formal Comment to Ecosphere, where you would be invited to reply. Either way, you see it first.

Let me know about July 8 and the data handoff, and I will coordinate with Regina and Jereme. Looking forward to it.

Aaron
